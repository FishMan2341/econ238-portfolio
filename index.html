Same Water, More "Contaminants"

What happens when chemical detection improves 1,000x? Hold the water constant and lower only the lab's detection limit. The number of chemicals "found" shoots up. The amount people are exposed to does not change at all.

Background: the same chemicals, as a better lab sweeps left. Detected: 0
The main finding

The same national survey, read at two detection limits

EPA's third Unregulated Contaminant Monitoring Rule (UCMR 3, 2013–2015) tested U.S. public water systems for six PFAS ("forever chemicals"). Labs could only report results above EPA's minimum reporting limits of 10–90 ppt. Andrew Eaton's lab re-reported its subset of about 10,500 UCMR 3 samples down to 5 ppt. Nothing about the water changed.

Percent of samples with the chemical "detected." Gray bars use the official UCMR 3 limits (shown in each label); colored bars use 5 ppt. PFBS at the official limit was <0.1% (plotted as 0.05%). Eaton reports that at 2.5 ppt, detection frequencies roughly doubled again.

What this shows

EPA's 2013–15 national drinking-water survey only reported PFAS above 10–90 ppt. Read the same survey down to 5 ppt and "detections" jump: PFOA from about 1% of samples to 12.5%, PFOS from 0.8% to 11.5%. Nothing about the water changed, only the instrument.

Over those same years, Americans' blood PFOS levels fell more than 85%. A rising count of "detected" contaminants can measure better labs, not more exposure. The question to ask is always: how much, compared with what health benchmark?

The other side: better detection isn't only noise. Today's 4 ppt PFOA/PFOS limit is below what the old survey could see, and EPA estimates about 7.8% of water systems exceed it. Detection reveals real risk up to a point; past that point it mostly adds headlines.

What 9 orders of magnitude of better detection buys you

Reading left to right, the lab gets better. The teal line keeps climbing for as long as detection improves. The red line stops once the detection limit falls below the health benchmarks. After that point, extra sensitivity adds headlines but no new risk information.

The 1,000x test: The water is identical, and the number of real exceedances doesn't change.
How the model works (so you can rebuild it)

There are 120 hypothetical chemicals. Each one's concentration is drawn from a lognormal distribution: log10(ppt) ~ Normal(mean −1, sd 2), so the median is 0.1 ppt. Each also gets its own health benchmark, log10(ppt) ~ Normal(2.5, 1.2), a median of about 300 ppt. A chemical is "detected" if its concentration ≥ the detection limit, and a "real exceedance" if its concentration > its benchmark. Total exposure is the sum of all 120 concentrations. A fixed random seed (2341) means everyone sees the same water.

Lognormal is the standard shape for environmental concentration data: many trace chemicals and a few at higher levels. The parameters are illustrative, not fitted to a specific dataset, so the shape of the result is the point, not the exact counts. Try changing them in the page source (CONC_MEAN, CONC_SD, BENCH_MEAN, BENCH_SD) to see what's robust. The detected count always rises as the limit falls. The risk count never does.

Detections went up while exposure went downWater systems with PFAS detected vs. PFAS in Americans’ blood

Water systems with any PFAS detected ▲

UCMR 3: 194 of 4,864 systems; reporting limits 10–90 ppt; 6 PFAS. UCMR 5: 3,309 of 9,876 systems in EPA's July 2025 interim summary; limits 2–20 ppt (4 ppt for PFOA and PFOS); 29 PFAS. Part of the jump comes from testing more compounds, which is why Exhibit 2, where only the limit changed, is the cleaner test.

PFAS in Americans' blood (median, ng/mL) ▼

CDC NHANES biomonitoring, women aged 16–49 (EPA indicator). Across the whole population, CDC reports blood PFOS fell more than 85% and PFOA more than 70% since 1999–2000, after U.S. manufacturers phased the two chemicals out.

But here's the other side. Better detection is not only noise. The 2024 EPA limit for PFOA and PFOS is 4 ppt, below what UCMR 3 could even report (20 and 40 ppt). The older survey could not see many systems that are now over the legal limit. EPA estimates about 7.8% of systems would exceed the PFOA or PFOS standard. In the model above, this is the stretch where the red line is still climbing: detection that reveals real risk. Past that point, detection keeps finding things that are probably not risks.
Detection limits have fallen about a billionfoldFrom the 1958 Delaney Clause to today’s 4 ppt limit

Log scale, so each step down is 1,000x. Points are documented benchmarks for different chemicals and labs, not a single continuous series.

  • 1958, Delaney Clause: no food additive shown to cause cancer may be used, at any level. "Zero" then meant "below about 1 ppm."
  • 1980s: FDA notes many chemicals can now be measured around 1 ppb. Zero tolerance becomes unworkable, and regulators move to risk thresholds such as a one-in-a-million lifetime cancer risk.
  • 2024: EPA's health goal for PFOA and PFOS is literally zero, so the enforceable limit (4 ppt) was set at the lowest level labs can reliably measure. The legal limit is defined by the instrument. As of mid-2026, EPA has kept the PFOA/PFOS limits (compliance pushed to 2031) and proposed rescinding limits on four other PFAS.
How small is a "part per trillion"?ppm, ppb, ppt and ppq explained as seconds of time

Concentrations in water are reported as parts per million (ppm), billion (ppb), trillion (ppt) and quadrillion (ppq). Each step is 1,000x smaller. Think of each "part" as one second.

UnitIn waterAs time: 1 second in…
1 ppm1 milligram per liter≈ 11.6 days
1 ppb1 microgram per liter≈ 31.7 years
1 ppt1 nanogram per liter≈ 31,700 years
1 ppq1 picogram per liter≈ 31.7 million years

The time comparisons are simple arithmetic: 106, 109, 1012 and 1015 seconds.

All five takeaways (and what this doesn’t claim)The full argument in five cards
1
"Detected" describes the lab, not the risk.

A rising count of contaminants can be produced entirely by better instruments, with no change in the water.

2
Dose is the risk question.

The question to ask about any "X found in Y" headline: at what concentration, compared with what health benchmark?

3
Sensitivity has diminishing returns for risk.

Improving detection pays off until the limit drops below the level that matters for health. After that, it mostly creates new "findings."

4
But the early gains were real.

PFAS shows that older limits sat above today's health benchmarks, so better detection uncovered real exposures. Detection is a tool. It is not the enemy.

5
The economics.

When a legal limit is pegged to what labs can detect, every improvement in chemistry can widen the regulated universe, along with testing, treatment and litigation costs, without new evidence of harm. Regulators have to decide where the marginal benefit of finding more stops being worth the marginal cost.

Not claimed here: that PFAS are harmless, or that any specific detection is safe. The claim is narrower: the count of detected contaminants, on its own, does not measure exposure or risk.

Methods, AI use, and how to extend thisWhat I asked the AI to do, the computations, and next questions

What I asked the AI (Claude) to do

  • Find national datasets where the same water was evaluated at different detection limits. This turned up Eaton's UCMR 3 re-analysis, which I then checked against EPA's official UCMR 3 reporting limits.
  • Pull detection rates from EPA's UCMR 3 and UCMR 5 summaries, biomonitoring trends from CDC/EPA, and the history of detection limits (BfR, FDA via Cato's Regulation).
  • Check the current legal status of the PFAS drinking-water rule (as of mid-2026).
  • Write the lognormal simulation and the interactive charts (Chart.js), then stress-test the framing. For example, I asked "isn't the UCMR 3 → UCMR 5 jump partly because they tested more chemicals?" That's why Exhibit 2 is the main real-data evidence and Exhibit 3 carries a caveat.

Computations

  • Percentages for UCMR 3 and UCMR 5 are counts divided by systems tested (194 / 4,864 = 4.0%; 3,309 / 9,876 = 33.5%).
  • All Exhibit 2 values are as reported by Eaton; I did no re-analysis of raw samples.
  • The simulation is fully in this page's source code. View source to see or modify it.

The next questions someone could ask

  • Run the same "fixed water, falling limit" test on microplastics or pharmaceuticals in water, where detection is improving fastest right now.
  • Pull UCMR 5 raw data (EPA posts it) and recompute detection rates as if the limits were UCMR 3's. That gives a direct apples-to-apples test on the newer survey.
  • Estimate the cost per microgram of exposure avoided as the treatment target moves from 70 ppt (EPA's 2016 health advisory, see Hu et al.) to 4 ppt.
