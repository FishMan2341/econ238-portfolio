More Americans, Fewer Storm Deaths Per Person

What 86 years of U.S. weather data say about adaptation

Deaths from tornadoes, floods and hurricanes held roughly steady while the population more than doubled. Heat is the exception, and how heat deaths are counted changes the answer.

Tornadoes killed about as many Americans in 2011 (553) as they did in 1953 (515).1 Read on its own, that looks like sixty years without progress. But the country nearly doubled in size over that time, from about 160 million people to 312 million.3 Roughly the same number of deaths spread across twice as many people means each person became far safer.

That is the central idea of this page: the number you divide by changes the conclusion. The sections below use official data to follow that idea through five findings.

  1. Storm deaths per person fell by about two-thirds
  2. Movers are heading to hotter counties and milder winters
  3. Reported heat deaths rose sharply in the 2020s
  4. “Temperature deaths” can mean 555 or 120,000
  5. Relocating coastal buildings would cost ~0.01% of GDP a year

1Storm deaths vs. population, 1940–2025

Storm deaths per person fell by about two-thirds

The National Weather Service (NWS) publishes annual deaths from tornadoes, floods and hurricanes back to 1940.1 Averaging each decade gives the chart below. Over the full period (1940–2025, 86 years, 20,676 deaths) the average is about 240 deaths per year. The decade averages move between roughly 170 and 310 with no clear direction, so the raw count alone suggests little has changed.

The population did not stay the same, though. Dividing each decade’s deaths by its average population gives a death intensity: deaths per million residents per year.1,3

Death intensityannual deaths ÷ average population × 1,000,000
1940s262.3 ÷ 139.7 M = 1.88 per million
2010s195.1 ÷ 320.6 M = 0.61 per million

Deaths per million fell from 1.88 in the 1940s to 0.61 in the 2010s, a 68% drop. In annual odds, that is roughly 1 in 530,000 falling to 1 in 1.6 million. Most of the decline came between the 1970s (1.41) and the 1980s (0.73), and the rate has stayed near 0.6–0.8 since.

Caveats. NWS counts come from Storm Data reports filed by local forecast offices. In the modern data the “hurricane” column counts wind deaths only; storm-surge and rain-flood deaths are filed under Flood.2 A single catastrophe can move a whole decade’s average. The CDC, not the NWS, is the official source for cause of death.2

Why did storm deaths per person fall?

This data can’t say which cause mattered most, but the likely candidates are higher incomes (sturdier housing and vehicles), better forecasts and warnings that reach more people, and stronger building codes and emergency management. One adaptation has been measured directly for a related hazard: the mortality effect of a very hot day fell about 80% between 1900–1959 and 1960–2004, and the spread of home air conditioning explains nearly all of that decline.13

Deaths and dollars also tell different stories. The IPCC’s special report on extreme events (SREX) concluded that rising economic losses from disasters are driven mainly by increasing exposure of people and property, and that long-term loss trends adjusted for wealth and population “have not been attributed to climate change,” though a role for climate change “has not been excluded.”14 More valuable property keeps being built in harm’s way even as fewer people die.

2Where Americans are moving, 2020–2025

Movers are heading to hotter counties and milder winters

The Census Bureau’s center-of-population map shows the country drifting south and west for two centuries,6 but an average location can’t show whether people are moving into more exposed places. To test that for extreme heat, I split 3,098 counties (about 98% of the 2020 population) into five groups of equal population, ranked by average July high temperature (NOAA, 1991–2020). For each group I summed net domestic migration from July 2020 to July 2025 (Census estimates). This measure counts only people moving within the U.S., not births, deaths or immigration.5,4

The hottest two fifths gained about 2.9 million net domestic movers in five years, while the cooler three lost about 2.8 million. The largest gainers include Collin and Montgomery counties in Texas, Polk and Pasco in Florida, and Pinal and Maricopa in Arizona, all with average July highs above 90°F.

The answer depends on scale. Some of the hottest big-city cores lost residents (Dallas County about −191,000; Miami-Dade about −271,000), many of them to nearby suburbs with the same climate. Running the same test on winter heating demand shows the other side: the fifth of counties with the mildest winters gained about 1.3 million movers.

What this means for the mortality story: migration raises Americans’ exposure to summer heat but lowers their exposure to cold. That complicates any simple verdict. Some of the rise in heat deaths (next section) may reflect where people now live, not only hotter weather. At the same time, moving away from cold may itself protect health: Deschênes and Moretti estimate that moves from the Northeast to the Southwest account for 4–7% of U.S. life-expectancy gains over thirty years.7

3Heat deaths, 1986–2025

Reported heat deaths rose sharply in the 2020s

The NWS began reporting heat deaths in 1986 and cold deaths in 1988.1 Heat deaths averaged about 115 a year in the 2000s and 2010s, then 382 a year in 2020–2025, more than tornadoes, floods and hurricanes combined (230 a year). Cold deaths run about 25–40 a year in NWS data. Once heat is included, total weather deaths are no longer clearly falling this decade.

The NWS also undercounts heat deaths. In 2023, Maricopa County, Arizona alone recorded 645 heat deaths,10 more than the NWS’s 555 for the entire country. CDC death certificates, a separate source, listed heat on 2,325 deaths that year, and age-adjusted heat death rates on death certificates rose about 16.8% a year from 2016 to 2023.9 Two independent sources pointing the same way suggest the recent rise is real, even though the NWS level is too low.

What the 1995 spike shows

Most of the 1,021 deaths in 1995 came from Chicago’s July heat wave, which caused at least 700 excess deaths.11 A CDC case-control study found the highest risk among people confined to bed, living alone, not leaving home each day, or living on a top floor. People with working air conditioners had about one-third the odds of dying.11 During another strong heat wave in 1999, after the city introduced interventions aimed at the elderly, at least 80 people died, and a working air conditioner was again the strongest protective factor.12 Heat deaths depend on exposure and vulnerability (isolation, housing, age, and access to cooling), not temperature alone. Those are factors policy can change.

4Counted vs. estimated deaths

“Temperature deaths” can mean 555 or 120,000

A 2025 study by Marshall Burke and co-authors, using decades of data from 30 countries, attributes about 5% of U.S. deaths and 12% of EU deaths to non-optimal temperatures: about 120,000 American deaths a year.8 That is roughly 180 times the NWS’s ten-year average for all weather deaths (658 a year). The two numbers are not in conflict. They measure different things.

Counted deaths vs. estimated deaths

  • Counted (NWS, CDC): individual deaths that a forecaster, coroner or doctor linked to the weather, such as heatstroke, hypothermia or a collapsed building.
  • Estimated (Burke): the extra deaths compared with a hypothetical where every day was at the temperature with the lowest mortality risk (about 20–25°C, or 68–77°F). Much of this shows up as cardiovascular and respiratory deaths, concentrated among older people, on records that never mention temperature. From 1968 to 2019, only 0.058% of U.S. death records mentioned temperature, nearly two orders of magnitude below the estimated burden.8
  • Cold matters more than heat in Burke’s sample: cold deaths outnumber heat deaths at least 5 to 1, and moderately cold days (5–10°C, or 41–50°F) cause the largest share.8
  • Timing differs. Heat produces an immediate spike followed by a return to normal, consistent with some deaths occurring only days earlier than they otherwise would have. Cold’s effects build over several weeks.8

Questions to ask of any temperature-death statistic: Was it counted or estimated? Heat, cold, or both? Compared with what baseline temperature?

For scale: if the 5–12% range applied to all 61.7 million deaths worldwide each year,20 temperature would account for 3.1 to 7.4 million deaths a year. A separate global study estimated about 5.1 million (9.4% of deaths, mostly cold).18 For the U.S., 5% of the 3.07 million deaths in 2024 is about 150,000.19 These are reasonable estimates of a real statistical burden, but they should never be compared directly with NWS counts.

5Costs of adapting

Relocating coastal buildings would cost ~0.01% of GDP a year

Risk comes from a hazard meeting exposure. We can’t weaken a hurricane, but we can move what sits in its path. Neither approach is automatically better. The right choice is whichever buys more safety per dollar at the margin.

The cost of moving to higher ground

Course figures based on William Nordhaus’s hurricane research put about 3% of the U.S. capital stock below 10 meters of elevation in Atlantic hurricane zones, including roughly $600 billion of vulnerable structures.15 Suppose none are torn down early, and each is rebuilt somewhere safer when it wears out:

$600B (assumed 2019 dollars) × CPI to 2025≈ $756 billion
Assumed 50-year lifetime → 2% replaced each year≈ $15.1 billion/yr
Assumed extra 25% to rebuild elsewhere (land, roads, utilities)≈ $3.8 billion/yr
Share of 2025 GDP ($30.8 trillion)≈ 0.012%

Under harsher assumptions (30-year lifetimes, relocation costing 100% of the building’s value) the total is about $25 billion a year, or 0.08% of GDP. Under milder ones (80-year lifetimes, a 10% premium) it is under $1 billion.17 On paper, gradual relocation is affordable. Yet Nordhaus found no indication of this kind of adaptation to date, and noted that damages from storms of a given strength have been trending up.16 A likely economic explanation is that the people deciding where to build don’t bear the full cost: coastal locations have real value to their owners, disaster aid and public flood insurance shift some losses to taxpayers, and local governments rely on coastal property taxes.

What reduces temperature deaths

Burke’s review finds that many common programs (heat alerts, cooling centers, urban greening) lack rigorous evidence, and that U.S. heat warnings are rarely issued on days below 30°C even though most heat deaths occur below that level.8 Several of the best-supported interventions are not climate policies: the 1960s–70s expansion of U.S. community health centers cut heat-related mortality by 14%, health insurance for low-income households in Mexico and Colombia cut the effect of moderate cold by at least half, and California’s outdoor-worker heat rules cut heat injuries in targeted industries by 75–90%.8

Evaluating one intervention: air conditioning. AC is strongly linked to lower heat mortality; Portuguese hospitals with AC had 40% lower mortality during the 2003 heat wave. But owning an air conditioner is not the same as running one, and low-income households run theirs less on hot days. Burke also lists higher EU energy costs as one possible reason Europe is more temperature-sensitive than the U.S.8 The economic questions are at what cost (equipment, electricity, peak grid capacity, emissions), compared with what (health care access and insulation, which also address cold), who decides (households, landlords, governments setting energy prices), and how we know (most AC evidence is correlational). AC likely saves lives on the hottest days, but it is one tool, not a complete plan.

6Limits of this analysis

What could make these conclusions wrong

Counting rules

Surge deaths are filed under Flood, and heat deaths are undercounted by the NWS. Reporting practices shape both trends.

Averages hide people

A falling death rate doesn’t tell us whether the next dollar of protection is worth spending. That is a marginal question.

Adaptation can stall

The U.S. temperature burden was the same share of deaths in the 2010s as in the 1980s, partly because the population is older.8

Cold vs. winter

Winter also brings flu season and indoor crowding. Separating the effect of a cold day from winter itself needs more evidence.

A big number is not a verdict. Neither is a small one.

Americans have become far better at surviving storms, even as they move toward heat. Most temperature deaths happen quietly, on ordinary cold days. The useful question is where the next dollar of protection saves the most lives, and the evidence points to exposure and vulnerability (where we build, who can cool or heat their home, and access to care) as much as to the weather itself.

Decades are calendar decades starting in 1940; the last group is 2020–2025 (six years, 2025 preliminary). Storm deaths are the NWS tornado + flood + hurricane columns. Population is the BEA midyear resident series averaged within each decade.
