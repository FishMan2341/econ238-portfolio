This is a headline featured in the August edition of the Campus times – in bright bold lettering on the main page. At first glance, 800,000 gallons sounds like an immense amount of water. The word “consume” makes it sound like the water disappears, and AI already has a reputation of being unwanted and disastrous. From an emotional standpoint, the verdict is simple: stop the project.

From an economic standpoint, 800,000 gallons is not a verdict. It is a data point.

For comparison, a 2020 University of Rochester utilities report outlined that the River Campus, Medical center, and Mid Campus system moved an estimated 250 million gallons of domestic water every year. This comes out to roughly 685,000 gallons per day. Yet, we do not portray this university as a water-devouring monster because we can clearly see and feel its upsides: education, research, jobs, and medical care. This comparison serves to demonstrate that a big number without context, although may sound scary, tells us nothing.

So, the first question we must ask when putting this number into context is: is the number a withdrawal, actual consumption, discharge, capacity, or permit limit? These are not interchangeable factors. A 2021 report by the New York Department of Environmental Conservation stated that the University’s permit allowed them up to 45 million gallons per day of non-contact cooling water discharge into the Genesee. This is a permitted maximum, not proof that the University destroyed 45 million gallons of drinking water every day.

The second question we should ask ourselves is whether the consumed resource is scarce in the area during a specific time frame. 800,000 gallons of water pulled from an already-declining aquifer is a very different case compared to the same withdrawal from a water-rich area where most of it is returned. Electricity is similar. A steady 150 megawatt load would use about 1.31 billion kilowatt hours if ran at full power all year - roughly six times the reported annual grid purchases of the University. There is no doubt that this is a big number, however the important question becomes which power plants have to work extra to supply this energy, especially during peak hours.

The third question is, who pays? In a 2025 update from LMGW - a utility provider, it was reported that xAI paid for the electrical upgrades that would be necessary in order for them to connect to the grid, paid a premium for energy, and agreed to reduce its consumption when demand was high. The update also highlighted a proposed recycled water facility for xAI and other industrial consumers. Furthermore, the organization underlined that it was their priority to ensure that a new customer does not affect the quality of service that the old customers receive. While these statements do matter, they don’t tell us whether these prices cover carbon emissions, local pollution, water scarcity, or the risk that ordinary citizens could inherit the cost of the data plant if the project leaves.

This brings us to the true problem unaccounted for in the market: an externality. A data center using a lot of electricity is not automatically a problem; after all, it still contributes to an effective market in which every action has a price. However, a data center making everyone else pay for its infrastructure, pollution, or a depleted water source is. The same standards hold true for any large-scale industrial project, whether it be a factory, hospital, or farm. The label on the building or the controversy surrounding it is much less important than where the actual costs go.

The fourth question we should ask is: what are the benefits and comparative costs of building? If we continue with the project, we will receive more jobs, government benefits, cheaper computing, and an influx of new infrastructure in the area. The immediate costs are likely more congestion, noise pollution, and land depreciation. Furthermore, we should factor the relative cost of using the resources on a project like this as opposed to something else. Could this labor be used in a more effective way doing something else? Is this the most efficient use of our land and water? We won't always have an answer for every question, yet it is useful to think of the benefits in terms of the cost.

The fifth question we should ask is: what happens next? If Rochester rejects a project, the demand for computing does not disappear. The facility would simply move to another location nearby that is willing to pay the price for building - one that may have more fragile resources and a less politically powerful or economically mindful population. Rochester’s immediate pollution levels may decline, sure, but the total levels in the area would still decrease. On the other hand, if Rochester approves everything and subsidizes the construction, it may start a “gold rush” within data centers looking for easy and cheap land to build on while not paying the proper price to the city’s inhabitants.

This is why neither a “ban it” or “build it” approach is a serious policy by itself.

And New York is taking positive steps in this direction. The state’s current one year pause establishes a “Temporary Moratorium on Data Centers in New York While the State Develops Higher Standards for Data Center Development,” holding certain permit applications with facilities using at least 50 megawatts while new rules are implemented. The next step should be clear rules; protect ordinary consumers from infrastructure costs created solely by one customer; protect people from equipment costs if the project leaves; charge more for utilities during droughts and peak hours; enforce pollution; set limits on noise; require developers to put real “skin in the game” with contracts rather than promises. Then, let the developers decide whether the project still makes sense.

We do not need another comparison to atomic bombs, olympic pools, or trips around the earth. We need clarity. We need to know what resources are used, what is actually scarce, who receives the benefits, who gets the bills, and where the project goes if we say no.

Eight hundred thousand gallons is a big number, no doubt about it. However, whether it’s a big problem is a completely different question.
