800,000 Gallons Sounds Terrifying. Here Are Some Questions to Ask Before You Panic.

At first glance, 800,000 gallons sounds like an immense amount of water. The word “consume” makes it sound as though the water disappears, and AI already has a reputation for being unwanted and disastrous. From an emotional standpoint, the verdict is simple: stop the project.

**From an economic standpoint, 800,000 gallons is not a verdict. It is a data point.**

## A big number needs context

For comparison, a [2020 University of Rochester utilities report](https://web.archive.org/web/20221221014415id_/https://www.facilities.rochester.edu/utilities/documents/Academic_Collaboration_Guide_Fall2020.pdf) estimated that the River Campus, Medical Center, and Mid Campus systems moved 250 million gallons of domestic water per year—roughly 685,000 gallons per day.[^1] Yet we do not portray the university as a water-devouring monster because we can clearly see its benefits: education, research, jobs, and medical care. A big number without context may sound frightening, but by itself it tells us very little.

## 1. What does the number measure?

Is 800,000 gallons a withdrawal, actual consumption, discharge, system capacity, or permit limit? These measures are not interchangeable.

For example, a [2021 notice from the New York State Department of Environmental Conservation](https://dec.ny.gov/news/environmental-notice-bulletin/2021-04-07/completed-application/university-of-rochester-central-utilities) stated that the University of Rochester’s permit allowed the discharge of up to 45 million gallons per day of non-contact cooling water into the Genesee River.[^2] That figure was a permitted maximum for discharge—not proof that the university destroyed 45 million gallons of drinking water every day.

## 2. Is the resource scarce here and now?

Eight hundred thousand gallons withdrawn from an already-declining aquifer is a very different case from the same withdrawal in a water-rich area where most of the water is returned. Location, season, source, and what happens after use all matter.

Electricity is similar. A steady 150-megawatt load would use about 1.31 billion kilowatt-hours if it ran at full power for a year—about 7.7 times the university report’s figure for annual electricity distribution across the three campus systems.[^1] That is unquestionably a large number. The important question, however, is which power plants would have to run more to supply the energy, especially during peak hours.

## 3. Who pays?

In a [2025 update from Memphis Light, Gas and Water](https://www.mlgw.com/images/content/files/pdf/new/xAI%202025%20Update.pdf), the utility reported that xAI paid for the electrical upgrades needed to connect its first 150 megawatts to the grid, paid the prevailing industrial electricity rate, and agreed to reduce its grid consumption when demand was high.[^3] The update also described a proposed recycled-wastewater facility for xAI and other industrial users. MLGW emphasized that adding a new customer should not reduce the quality of service for existing customers.

Those commitments matter, but they do not tell us whether the prices cover carbon emissions, local pollution, water scarcity, or the risk that ordinary residents could inherit stranded infrastructure costs if the data center leaves.

This is the market problem we should focus on: an **externality**. A data center’s high electricity use is not automatically a market failure; in a functioning market, resource use has a price. But it is a problem when a data center makes everyone else pay for its infrastructure, pollution, or depletion of a shared water source. The same standard should apply to any large industrial project, whether it is a factory, hospital, farm, or data center. The label on the building—and the controversy surrounding it—matters less than where the actual costs go.

## 4. What are the benefits and opportunity costs?

Building the project could bring jobs, economic activity, cheaper computing, and new infrastructure. The immediate costs could include congestion, noise pollution, and declining nearby land values.

We should also ask what else could be done with the same resources. Could the labor be used more effectively elsewhere? Is this the best use of the region’s land, water, and electric capacity? We will not always have a precise answer, but we should still weigh the benefits against both the direct costs and the opportunities we give up.

## 5. What happens if Rochester says no?

If Rochester rejects a project, the demand for computing does not disappear. The facility may simply move to another community—possibly one with more fragile resources or less political power to negotiate protections. Rochester’s immediate pollution may decline, but the pollution could merely shift elsewhere.

On the other hand, if Rochester approves every proposal and subsidizes construction, it could start a “gold rush” among data-center developers looking for inexpensive land without paying the full cost imposed on residents.

## Beyond “ban it” or “build it”

Neither “ban it” nor “build it” is a serious policy by itself.

New York has begun moving in a more useful direction. In July 2026, [Executive Order 62](https://www.governor.ny.gov/executive-order/no-62-establishing-temporary-moratorium-data-centers-new-york-while-state-develops) established a temporary moratorium on certain state environmental permits for new hyperscale data centers demanding at least 50 megawatts while the state develops stronger standards.[^4]

The next step should be clear rules: protect ordinary consumers from infrastructure costs created by a single customer; guard against stranded equipment costs if a project leaves; charge more for utilities during droughts and peak-demand hours; enforce pollution and noise limits; and require developers to put real “skin in the game” through contracts rather than promises. Then let developers decide whether the project still makes sense.

We do not need another comparison to atomic bombs, Olympic pools, or trips around the Earth. We need clarity. We need to know what resources are used, what is actually scarce, who receives the benefits, who gets the bills, and where the project goes if we say no.

Eight hundred thousand gallons is a big number, no doubt about it. Whether it is a big problem is a completely different question.
